\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This report combined analytical calculations, process and device simulations, cleanroom processing, and electrical measurements to study the BICMOS5 process and the behaviour of the fabricated NMOS and PMOS devices. Overall, the results show good qualitative agreement between theory, Sentaurus simulations, and the experimental data, while the remaining differences can be explained by simplifying assumptions in the analytical models, the full thermal budget of the real process, and measurement-structure-dependent effects.

From the technology assignments and process simulations, the main trends of implantation, diffusion, and masking were confirmed. In crystalline silicon, channeling strongly affects implantation depth and profile tails, whereas in amorphous silicon the dopant profiles become much closer to Gaussian. The simulations also showed that arsenic is the most suitable dopant for shallow, highly doped NMOS source/drain regions, while phosphorus is more suitable for the N-Well because it diffuses deeper into the substrate. In addition, the masking simulations showed that a thin ($0.4\,\mu\text{m}$) photoresist layer is clearly insufficient for the chosen phosphorus implant and that an equally thick oxide layer only just stops it, while nitride provides the highest stopping power per unit thickness, although it is impractical as a standard implantation mask; in practice a sufficiently thick photoresist layer is used.

Comparison of the analytical junction-depth calculations with the Sentaurus process simulation showed clear differences. The simulated junction depths were approximately $0.537\,\mu\text{m}$ for SN, $2.085\,\mu\text{m}$ for the N-Well, and $0.594\,\mu\text{m}$ for SP, whereas the simple calculations of Section~\ref{sec:tech} predicted much shallower SN and SP junctions ($0.087$ and $0.186\,\mu\text{m}$) and a slightly deeper N-Well ($2.37\,\mu\text{m}$). This difference is expected because the analytical treatment assumes simplified Gaussian or limited-source profiles and neglects the later thermal steps of the full CMOS process and the solid solubility limit, while the simulation includes the dopant redistribution during oxidation and annealing. Only for the N-Well, where the long drive-in dominates, is the analytical estimate close.

The processing assignments confirmed the expected behaviour of oxide growth, etching, and metal patterning. Wet oxidation produced a denser and more uniform oxide than PECVD, and the PECVD oxide etched about $3.2$ times faster in buffered HF ($5.53$ versus $1.71$ nm/s) because of its lower density and porosity. For the aluminium etch, wet etching showed very high selectivity to the underlying oxide but caused isotropic underetching, whereas dry etching gave straighter and better-defined metal lines at the cost of much lower oxide selectivity. These observations are consistent with the fundamental trade-off between selectivity and dimensional control in wet and dry processing.

The electrical measurements validated both the processing and the simulation results. Van der Pauw measurements gave sheet resistances of about $1740\,\Omega/\square$ for the N-Well, $60.2\,\Omega/\square$ for SN, and $488\,\Omega/\square$ for SP, within $5$ to $17\%$ of the in-line four-point-probe values, and the aluminium interconnect measured $0.16\,\Omega/\square$, corresponding to a resistivity of $3.2\times10^{-6}\,\Omega\,\text{cm}$, the literature value for Al with $1\%$ Si. The average active dopant concentrations extracted through the Irvin curves ($1.5\times10^{16}$, $2.3\times10^{19}$ and $1.6\times10^{18}\,\text{cm}^{-3}$ for N-Well, SN and SP) agree with the Section~\ref{sec:processing} values to within about $10\%$, and are only consistent with the simulated junction depths, not the analytical ones. The ELM measurements showed clear lateral out-diffusion, which was small for SN, about $0.15\,\mu\text{m}$ per side, but much larger for the N-Well, on the order of $0.8$ to $2.1\,\mu\text{m}$ per side, consistent with the longer and hotter phosphorus drive-in. This confirms that lateral diffusion becomes increasingly important for lightly doped, deeply driven layers.

The transistor measurements also matched the expected device physics. For long-channel devices, the extracted threshold voltages and mobilities remained nearly constant with geometry, confirming that they are process rather than device parameters, with electron mobilities around $750$--$800\,\text{cm}^2/\text{Vs}$ and hole mobilities around $230$--$300\,\text{cm}^2/\text{Vs}$, both below bulk values as expected for surface-channel conduction. The short-channel ($L = 1\,\mu\text{m}$) devices showed threshold-voltage roll-off, an artificially inflated apparent mobility, roughly ten times stronger channel-length modulation, velocity-saturation signatures in the normalised output curves, and in the PMOS case the onset of punch-through, all in agreement with the trends predicted by device theory and the simulations.

Finally, the influence of the $V_\mathrm{T}$-adjust implant was clearly demonstrated. Increasing the boron dose from $0$ to $9\times10^{11}\,\text{cm}^{-2}$ increased the measured NMOS threshold voltage monotonically from about $-0.034\,\text{V}$ to $0.819\,\text{V}$, while the PMOS threshold voltage became less negative, changing from about $-3.702\,\text{V}$ to $-3.300\,\text{V}$. The Sentaurus device simulations reproduce both trends and the dose sensitivity, with a systematic offset of about $0.5$ to $0.6\,\text{V}$ that is attributed to the different threshold-extraction criteria and to the oxide and interface charge that is not included in the simulation. This confirms the theoretical expectation that a single unmasked boron implant raises the channel acceptor concentration for the NMOS and partially compensates the N-Well surface doping for the PMOS. Therefore, the measurements, simulations, and theoretical analysis consistently show how implantation and thermal processing determine the final electrical behaviour of the BICMOS5 devices.
